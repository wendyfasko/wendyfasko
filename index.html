English · Literary / philosophical essay · Work in progress
[starting point: time travel. actual problem: irreversibility.]

The Children Who Tried to Escape Time

What if the fantasy of time travel is really the fantasy that consequence can be revised?

Beginning with science fiction as a point of departure, this essay follows a much older literary problem: the discrepancy between chronological time and the way human beings actually inhabit it. Augustine, Bergson, Proust, Borges, Thomas Mann, Vonnegut, Sebald, Márquez and Gospodinov become different ways of thinking about memory, duration, trauma, repetition, nostalgia and the unbearable fact that a life cannot be edited backwards.

The question that survives the time machine is the one literature has been asking all along: how do we remain in a present that cannot be corrected?

03 / Fiction

turn up.

Long-form fiction about family, Albania, political inheritance, mortality and the inconvenient persistence of memory.

Novel · Written in Italian · Under contract
[family history has terrible boundaries]

TELOMERI

A man spent his life serving a regime. Only when he is dying does he realise the regime may have been the least complicated thing about it.

Set in Albania between the communist 1980s and 2023, Telomeri follows Sandri Golemi, former Communist Party secretary of the Berat district, as a rare cancer forces him to look back at a life surrendered to political cowardice, paranoia and the consequences of choices made under dictatorship.

Agnesa, his marriage, doubts over his daughter's paternity and the loss of his childhood friend Papi turn political history into domestic damage. Years later Sandri leaves Albania for the first time to find Papi in America. He arrives too late.

The dictatorship eventually falls. Its citizens still have to live with what it taught them.

The living move forward. The dead get closer.

Why Telomeri? Telomeres shorten as cells divide. In the novel, distance behaves similarly: as Sandri approaches death, the distances separating him from the people he has lost begin to contract.

Language: the novel is written in Italian while its narrative and historical world is Albanian. That movement — carrying Albania into Italian — later reverses in my translation practice, where I bring literature written elsewhere directly into Albanian.

04 / Translation

What survives when
a sentence changes language?

I grew up between Albanian and Italian. Translation turned that biographical accident into a working question: what happens when languages meet directly, without asking a larger language to supervise the conversation?

Current project · Norwegian → Albanian · 2026
[translation note № 04: repetition is not a problem to solve]

Johan Harstad
Max, Mischa & Tetoffensiven

I started translating Harstad for a fairly selfish reason. I wanted to hear what he sounded like in Albanian.

Harstad's prose accumulates, digresses and repeats itself until repetition becomes structure. The problem is not how to make him beautiful in Albanian. He already knows what he is doing. The problem is knowing when not to improve him.

The sample is translated directly from Norwegian, without an intermediary language, and is being developed for potential presentation to publishers in Albania and Kosovo, subject to rightsholder clearance.

Lexical architecture

hjem → shtëpi

Home. A very ordinary word until a thousand-page novel keeps asking where it is.

Preserving recurring lexical networks matters more to me than elegantly varying them away.

Keep the strangeness. Remove only the strangeness caused by passing from one language into another.

Norwegian Journalism and the Politics of Linguistic Survival

A journalist sits down to write about a technology that did not exist five years ago. English already has the word. Norwegian has a decision to make.

The project treats language as journalistic infrastructure and asks how digital journalism can help Norwegian remain not merely alive, but capable of naming contemporary public reality in an English-dominated technological environment. It considers newsroom terminology, technology and science reporting, platforms, search, AI-assisted writing, and the unequal digital conditions of Bokmål and Nynorsk.

Can a language preserve its past perfectly well while gradually losing the ability to describe its future?

ORBITALKORA

The Energy Operating System for Humans.

I got tired of software behaving as though every human wakes up with the same brain, body, energy and capacity every morning. So I designed the opposite.

OrbitalKora is an accessibility-first digital system built around changing energy, attention, executive function and capacity. I developed its product concept, information architecture, interaction model, feature system, ethical framework, business strategy and initial functional prototype.

The recurring problem

Systems are never neutral.

Whether the system is a newsroom, a platform, a language, a body or a piece of software, I keep ending up in the same place: who was it designed for, what does it assume, and who has to adapt when those assumptions are wrong?

10 / Who is Wendy?

And why is she
doing all this?

I am Italian-Albanian, which means the question “where are you from?” has never been particularly efficient.

I studied literature because I wanted to understand stories, Digital Humanities because apparently literature was not complicated enough, journalism because I wanted to ask questions in public, and clean technologies because at some point nobody stopped me.

I became a novelist first. Then a literary critic. Then a journalist. Then I started building things. Translation came from the same place everything else did: language, migration, literature, and a tendency to follow a question considerably further than necessary.

There is a logic to this. I promise.

Most of my work eventually comes back to the same problem: what happens to human beings when they have to live inside systems — political, linguistic, technological, biological — that were not quite designed for them?

Sometimes the answer is an essay. Sometimes a novel. Sometimes software. Occasionally it takes more than a thousand pages and is written in Norwegian.
